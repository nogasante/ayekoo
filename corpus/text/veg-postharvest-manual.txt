The Sustainable Intensification of Mixed Farming Systems Initiative aims to provide 
equitable, transformative pathways for improved livelihoods of actors in mixed 
farming systems through sustainable intensification within target agroecologies and 
socio-economic settings. 
 
Through action research and development partnerships, the Initiative will improve 
smallholder farmers' resilience to weather-induced shocks, provide a more stable 
income and significant benefits in welfare, and enhance social justice and inclusion 
for 13 million people by 2030. 
 
Activities will be implemented in six focus countries globally representing diverse 
mixed farming systems as follows: Ghana (cereal–root crop mixed), Ethiopia 
(highland mixed), Malawi: (maize mixed), Bangladesh (rice mixed), Nepal (highland 
mixed), and Lao People's Democratic Republic (upland intensive mixed/ highland 
extensive mixed).

1. General Introduction to the Project and Its 
Intentions for the Trainees 
Welcome to the training program as part of our collaborative project, which is being 
implemented by the Department of Food Science and Postharvest Technology at 
Wachemo University in partnership with the Alliance of Bioversity International 
and CIAT  under the MFS initiative . This project is specifically designed for the 
communities in the Adoye and Shurmo clusters of Central Ethiopia. 
 
1.1. Objective of the Training 
 
The primary goal of this training manual is to provide farmers, development agents, 
and local stakeholders with practical knowledge and skills to enhance food security 
and improve community nutrition. It emphasizes essential areas such as postharvest 
handling of home garden produce and diversified food preparation and consumption 
pattern. By promoting best practices and offering clear, actionable guidance, the 
manual seeks to support the production of diverse, nutritious foods, reduce 
postharvest losses, and boost overall dietary diversity in the project areas. 
 
1.2. Project Background 
 
The Alliance of Bioversity and CIAT, under the MFS initiative, has been implementing 
integrated home garden intensification in Central Ethiopia, particularly in the 
Doyogena and Lemo districts. This initiative aims to improve livelihoods by integrating 
improved avocado, vegetables, and poultry production into home gardens. By 
diversifying home garden outputs, the strategy supports improved dietary diversity, 
nutrition, and household food security, especially in the densely populated Ethiopian 
highlands. Surplus produce from these intensified gardens not only improves family 
diets but also generates additional income for women and youth through local 
market sales, further strengthening household economic stability. 
To maximize the benefits of home garden intensification, proper postharvest 
handling is essential to deliver fresh, high-quality products to markets while ensuring 
                                                                                               
 2 
improved food preparation and consumption patterns at the household level. This 
ensures both food availability and nutritional security. Capacity-building programs for 
development agents (DAs) and farmers in postharvest handling, as well as raising 
awareness about improved food preparation techniques, are crucial to achieving the 
broader objectives of this initiative. Such efforts will help farmers fully benefit from 
intensified home gardens, thereby boosting food security and household income. 
Postharvest handling is critical to improving food security and nutrition, especially in 
regions like Asia and Sub-Saharan Africa, including Ethiopia, where postharvest losses 
are high. A significant portion of food is lost after harvest due to inadequate handling, 
poor storage, and limited processing techniques (Makiso et al., 2024) . Effective 
postharvest handling for home garden produce is key to reducing spoilage and 
preserving the nutritional value of fruits, vegetables, grains, and other crops. In 
Ethiopia alone, postharvest losses could feed over 23 million people annually, with an 
economic impact exceeding 1.2 billion US dollars (Teferra, 2022). Adopting improved 
storage methods such as drying, cooling, and better packaging can extend the shelf 
life of produce, reduce food waste, and maintain year-round access to fresh, nutrient-
dense foods. This directly enhances household food security by ensu ring a steady 
supply of fresh produce, which is often lacking in resource-limited areas. 
In countries like Ethiopia, where malnutrition and low dietary diversity are persistent 
challenges, enhancing the preservation and effective use of home garden produce 
can significantly improve nutrition outcomes. Poor postharvest practices often result 
in nutrient loss, which diminishes the health benefits of fresh produce. By improving 
storage and handling techniques, communities can not only reduce food waste but 
also retain essential nutrients, promoting a more diverse and nutritious diet. This 
enables families to include a wider variety of fruits, vegetables, and grains in their 
meals, addressing micronutrient deficiencies and boosting overall health. 
Furthermore, educating households on proper food preparation methods —such as 
balanced meal planning and cooking techniques that preserve nutrients—can further 
enhance dietary diversity and contribute to reducing malnutrition. 
This training manual is part of a broader initiative led by the Alliance of Bioversity 
International and CIAT under the MFS initiative, in partnership with the Department

of Food Science and Post-Harvest Technology at Wachemo University. The project is 
being implemented in the Adoye and Shurmo clusters of Kambata and Hadiya Zones, 
Central Ethiopia region. It is focused on improving postharvest handling of home 
garden produc ts, diversifying diet, balanced meal planning, preparation, and 
consumption through providing comprehensive guidance to development agents 
and farmers to ensure food security and better nutrition in the region. 
 
1.3. Target Audience 
 
This manual is designed for a diverse group of users, each playing a crucial role in 
improving nutrition and food security within their communities. To ensure better 
understanding and accessibility, the training content was translated into Amharic, 
enabling effective communication with farmers and other stakeholders. The key 
target audiences include: 
• Farmers: As the primary food producers, farmers will gain valuable insights into 
home gardening techniques, effective postharvest handling, and nutrition -
focused food preparation, empowering them to produce and utilize more 
nutritious foods. 
• Development Agents (DAs): Development agents will use the manual to train 
and support farmers, ensuring the adoption of best practices in postharvest 
handling and meal preparation, ultimately improving food security across the 
region. 
• Community Leaders : Community leaders will play a vital role in fostering 
ongoing engagement and support by sharing knowledge, advocating for 
better nutrition and food security practices, and promoting the adoption of 
sustainable agricultural practices within the community. 
• Household Members: Those responsible for food preparation will learn how to 
incorporate diverse, nutrient -rich foods into daily meals using local resources 
and produce from home gardens, enhancing household dietary diversity.

to achieving the project’s goals of improving food security and nutritional outcomes

3.1. Objective 
 
The primary objective of this training manual is to equip development agents and 
farmers in the Adoye and Shurmo clusters of Central Ethiopia region with essential 
knowledge and practical skills for effective postharvest handling of home garden 
products. By focusing on reducing food losses through better harvesting, storage, and 
preservation techniques, the manual aims to maintain the nutritional quality and 
freshness of locally grown fruits, vegetables, and grains. Emphasizing affordable and 
locally availa ble solutions, such as drying, cooling, and improved packaging, the 
manual seeks to extend the shelf life of home -grown produce, thereby improving 
household food security and income generation. It also promotes sustainable 
practices by strengthening the ca pacity of development agents, enabling them to 
train and support farmers in minimizing postharvest losses and enhancing 
marketable quality. Ultimately, this manual contributes to the larger goal of ensuring 
year-round access to nutritious foods while impro ving both livelihoods and food 
security in these rural communities.

handling practices with a focus on home garden products in the Adoye and Shurmo 
clusters, Central Ethiopia region. Proper postharvest handling of home garden

Postharvest losses are particularly high in Ethiopia due to improper handling, poor 
storage practices, and a lack of access to preservation techniques. These losses not

3.3. Key Principles of Postharvest Handling 
A. Timely Harvesting 
• Why it matters : Harvesting at the right time ensures optimal flavor, texture, 
and nutritional content. 
• Best Practices: 
o Vegetables: Harvest in the early morning or late afternoon when 
temperatures are cooler to preserve freshness. 
o Fruits: Pick fruits like avocado when they have reached the proper stage 
of maturity but before they are fully ripe. 
o Roots and Tubers: Harvest root crops like potatoes when they have fully 
matured and avoid damaging the skin. 
B. Sorting and Grading 
• Why it matters : Sorting and grading help identify produce that is fit for 
immediate consumption, storage, or sale. 
• Best Practices: 
o Remove damaged or overripe products that might spoil the rest. 
o Sort produce based on size, quality, and ripeness to streamline 
packaging and storage. 
C. Washing and Cleaning 
• Why it matters : Proper washing removes dirt, insects, and contaminants, 
reducing the risk of spoilage and disease. 
• Best Practices: 
o Use clean water for washing and gently remove any dirt. Avoid excessive 
washing, especially for products like potatoes, which can lead to 
waterlogging. 
o Allow produce to dry before storage to avoid moisture-related spoilage. 
D. Cooling and Shade 
• Why it matters : High temperatures accelerate spoilage, particularly for leafy 
vegetables and fruits. 
• Best Practices: 
                                                                                               
 19 
o Store produce in shaded areas immediately after harvest to reduce 
exposure to heat. 
o Use simple, low-cost cooling systems such as evaporative cooling using 
damp cloths or clay pots to keep the temperature low. 
3.4. Specific Postharvest Handling Techniques for Home Garden 
Products 
A. Green and Leafy Vegetables (Cabbage, Carrot, Endive, Tomato) 
• Harvesting: Leafy vegetables should be harvested in the morning when the 
water content is high. Careful handling during harvest is crucial to prevent 
bruising. 
• Sorting and Grading: Immediately after harvesting, remove damaged or wilted 
leaves. Keep only the healthy, fresh produce. 
• Storage: Vegetables like cabbage and carrots should be stored in cool, shaded 
areas. If refrigeration is unavailable, consider evaporative cooling (e.g., placing 
vegetables in a basket covered with wet cloths). 
• Transportation: Use ventilated baskets or sacks to transport vegetables to local 
markets or homes, ensuring minimal physical damage. 
B. Fruits (Avocado) 
• Harvesting: Harvest avocados at the right stage of ripeness to ensure the best 
flavour and shelf life. Use a sharp tool to avoid bruising the fruit. 
• Sorting and Grading : Immediately sort avocados based on size and ripeness. 
Separate those for immediate consumption from those that can be stored for 
later ripening. 
• Storage: Avocados should be kept in a cool, shaded area. Avoid direct sunlight 
and keep them in well-ventilated containers. 
• Transportation: Use padding (such as straw or banana leaves) to transport 
avocados to prevent bruising and damage during transport. 
C. Root and Tuber Crops (Potato, Beetroot, Enset) 
• Harvesting: Harvest potatoes and beetroots when the leaves have dried, 
indicating maturity. For Enset, cut the mature plants carefully to avoid 
damaging the roots. 
• Curing: Cure potatoes and beetroots in a shaded, dry place for a few days to 
heal minor cuts and prolong their storage life. 
                                                                                               
 20 
• Storage: Store root crops in a cool, well -ventilated area, such as an 
underground pit or a raised platform. Enset can be stored in underground pits 
for extended periods, where it ferments and serves as a food reserve during 
times of scarcity. 
• Transportation: Avoid stacking roots and tubers tightly to prevent bruising and 
spoilage. 
D. Cereals and Grains (Maize, Teff, Wheat) 
• Harvesting: Grains should be harvested when they have fully dried on the stalk. 
Avoid harvesting during rainy or wet conditions. 
• Drying: Ensure grains are thoroughly dried to a moisture content below 12%. 
Spread them out in the sun for several days, turning them regularly to ensure 
even drying. 
• Storage: Store grains in airtight containers or bags to prevent moisture and 
pest infestation. Raised platforms or metal silos can be used to keep grains off 
the ground. 
• Aflatoxin Control: Regularly check grains for mold or fungal growth, as these 
can lead to aflatoxin contamination, which is harmful to health.

3.5. Low-Cost Storage Solutions  
In rural areas where access to refrigeration or advanced storage technologies is 
limited, there are simple and affordable storage solutions that can help extend the 

                                                                                               
 21 
shelf life of home garden products. Each method offers specific advantages but also 
has limitations, and the duration of food preservation varies depending on the storage 
method and conditions. 
A. Raised Platforms 
• A simple raised platform made from locally available materials (wood or 
bamboo) helps keep produce off the ground, improving airflow and reducing 
spoilage. 
• Storage Duration : Typically extends shelf life by 3 –7 days for fruits and 
vegetables that benefit from improved ventilation. 
• Limitations: 
o Susceptible to pests and rodents unless properly secured. 
o Limited protection from rain or extreme weather conditions. 
o Not suitable for highly perishable items that require cooling. 
• Procedure: 
1. Collect locally available materials like bamboo or wooden planks. 
2. Build a raised platform about 30–50 cm above the ground using wooden posts 
as supports. 
3. Ensure the surface has slats or gaps to improve airflow. 
4. Place produce on the platform in a single layer to avoid crushing and ensure 
even ventilation. 
B. Underground Storage Pits 
• For root crops like potatoes and enset, underground pits lined with straw or 
banana leaves provide a cool, humid environment that preserves crops for 
several months. 
• Storage Duration: Can preserve root crops for 2 –3 months, depending on the 
type of crop and pit conditions. 
• Limitations: 
o Risk of contamination or spoilage if the lining materials (e.g., straw or 
banana leaves) are not clean or properly maintained. 
o Potential for waterlogging during heavy rains, which can damage stored 
crops. 
o Limited capacity and labor-intensive construction. 
                                                                                               
 22 
 
Figure 8: Underground potato storage pits 
• Procedure: 
1. Dig a circular or rectangular pit about 20 –50 cm deep, depending on the 
amount of produce (Figure 8). 
2. Line the pit with clean, dry straw, dried grass, or banana leaves to prevent direct 
contact between the crops and soil. 
3. Place root crops like potatoes or enset in a single layer on top of the lining. 
4. Cover the crops with another layer of straw or banana leaves. 
5. Seal the pit with soil, leaving a small vent for air circulation if needed. 
C. Clay Pot Coolers 
• A clay pot cooler (using two clay pots of different sizes, with wet sand in 
between) provides a low -cost, effective cooling system. This can be used to 
store fruits and vegetables, keeping them fresh for a longer period. 
• Storage Duration : Extends freshness of perishable produce for 7 –20 days, 
depending on ambient temperature and maintenance. 
 
 

                                                                                               
 23 
• Limitations: 
o Requires regular maintenance, such as keeping the sand moist, to 
maintain the cooling effect. 
o Limited storage space, making it impractical for larger quantities. 
o Effectiveness can be reduced in areas with low humidity or high 
temperatures. 
• Procedure: 
1. Obtain two clay pots, one smaller and one larger. 
2. Place the smaller pot inside the larger one, leaving a gap between them. 
3. Fill the gap with clean, moist sand. 
4. Add water to the sand regularly to keep it wet. 
5. Place fruits or vegetables inside the smaller pot and cover it with a damp cloth 
or lid. 
6. Position the cooler in a shaded, well -ventilated area to maximize its cooling 
effect. 
D. Evaporative Cooling Structures 
• Using a simple structure covered with wet clothes or sacks creates an 
evaporative cooling effect that can reduce the temperature of stored 
vegetables by several degrees. 
• Storage Duration: Can keep vegetables fresh for 5 –15 days, depending on the 
environmental conditions and crop type. 
• Limitations: 
o Requires constant access to water to keep the covering materials wet, 
which may be a challenge in water-scarce areas. 
o Effectiveness is significantly reduced in regions with high humidity 
where evaporation rates are low. 
o Limited durability of materials like sacks or cloth, which may degrade 
over time. 
• Procedure: 
1. Build a simple frame using wooden posts or bamboo sticks. 
2. Surround the frame with clean, wet cloths, gunny sacks, or similar materials. 
3. Place a flat platform or shelf inside the structure for storing produce. 
                                                                                               
 24 
4. Keep the covering materials consistently wet by sprinkling water several times 
a day. 
5. Place the structure in a shaded, breezy area to enhance the cooling effect. 
 
3.6. Training and Awareness for Postharvest Handling 
 
3.6.1. Training Development Agents (DAs) 
Development agents play a critical role in disseminating postharvest handling 
knowledge to farmers. They should be trained in the following areas: 
• Practical demonstrations of sorting, grading, and storage techniques. 
• How to organize community-based workshops and training sessions for 
farmers. 
• Monitoring and evaluating the adoption of postharvest practices among 
farmers. 
3.6.2. Training Farmers 
Farmers should be given hands-on experience in applying the postharvest techniques 
described above. Key areas of focus include: 
• Proper harvesting and storage methods to minimize losses. 
• How to use locally available materials for storage and transportation. 
• The importance of food safety and hygiene in postharvest handling. 
3.6.3. Raising Awareness 
Postharvest losses often occur due to a lack of awareness about simple, effective 
handling techniques. Raising awareness in the community through field days, 
demonstrations, and local leaders can help encourage farmers to adopt these 
methods. 
3.7. Practical Steps to Reduce Postharvest Losses 
 
Here are practical steps that farmers and development agents in Adoye and Shurmo 
clusters can take to reduce postharvest losses: 
A. Timely Harvesting : Ensure that crops are harvested at the right stage of 
maturity to prevent spoilage. 
B. Immediate Sorting : Sort and grade produce immediately after harvest to 
separate damaged or low-quality items. 
                                                                                               
 25 
C. Improved Storage: Use raised platforms, underground pits, or clay pot coolers 
to store produce in a cool, dry environment. 
D. Hygiene and Safety: Always maintain cleanliness during postharvest handling 
to prevent contamination and spoilage.

4. Dietary diversification of home garden produces 
 
4.1. Objective 
 
This training manual aims to promote dietary diversity and improve nutrition among 
farmers and household members in the Adoye and Shurmo clusters in Central 
Ethiopia region by focusing on sustainable, locally sourced food preparation practices. 
By teaching participants, the importance of balanced meals and effective meal 
planning, the manual seeks to combat malnutrition and address common 
micronutrient deficiencies in the community. It highlights the nutritional value  of 
locally available food items —such as green leafy vegetables, root crops, fruits, dairy, 
and eggs —and provides guidance on preserving their nutrients through proper 
cooking methods. Additionally, the manual empowers development agents to 
support farmers in creating more varied, nutritious mea ls using home -grown 
produce, reducing reliance on processed foods. By integrating these practices into 
daily consumption patterns, the manual contributes to long -term improvements in 
community health and food security, while respecting local traditions and  food 
preferences. 
 
4.2. Introduction 
 
Dietary diversity is key to improving food security and reducing malnutrition in rural 
communities like Adoye and Shurmo clusters. This training manual focuses on 
equipping development agents (DAs) and farmers with the knowledge and skills to 
prepare and c onsume diverse, nutrient -rich foods that are locally available and 
produced from home gardens. This manual aligns with the broader objectives of 
promoting better nutrition, food security, and overall well -being through a holistic 
approach that includes home gardening, proper postharvest handling, and nutritious

4.3. Nutritional Importance of Dietary Diversity 
 
In Ethiopia, many rural households rely on a limited range of staple foods, often 
resulting in low dietary diversity and micronutrient deficiencies. Diversifying diets by 
incorporating a variety of food groups —vegetables, fruits, grains, roots, animal 
products—helps ensure that families receive all essential nutrients such as vitamins, 
minerals, proteins, and healthy fats. 
By consuming a wider variety of foods, communities can: 
• Prevent Malnutrition : Combat both undernutrition and micronutrient 
deficiencies (e.g., iron, vitamin A). 
• Improve Immunity : A varied diet strengthens the immune system, helping 
prevent diseases. 
• Enhance Energy Levels : Balanced nutrition supports energy and overall 
physical well-being. 
• Boost Cognitive Function : Essential nutrients are important for cognitive 
development and performance.

4.5. Principles of Diversified Food Preparation 
 
The key to creating a nutritious and diversified meal is to include a balance of food 
groups from locally available resources. This ensures families get essential 
macronutrients (carbohydrates, proteins, fats) and micronutrients (vitamins and 
minerals). Basic principles include: 
• Variety: Include different types of food in each meal (vegetables, fruits, proteins, 
grains). 
• Seasonality: Use fresh, seasonal produce to maximize nutrient content. 
                                                                                               
 30 
• Balance: Ensure meals provide adequate energy (from carbohydrates), muscle 
support (from proteins), and essential fats. 
• Simplicity: Use traditional recipes but incorporate nutrient -dense ingredients 
for more balanced meals. 
 
4.6. Meal Planning for Nutritional Balance 
 
Meal planning helps households ensure that each meal includes a combination of 
essential nutrients. Development agents and farmers should be trained to use meal 
planning strategies to: 
• Incorporate local ingredients : Make use of home garden produce and local 
market foods. 
• Include all food groups : Ensure that meals contain grains or tubers, 
vegetables, fruits, and animal products. 
• Focus on portion sizes : Balance the quantity of food groups according to 
household size and activity level. 
Example of a Balanced Meal: 
• Staple: Injera (Teff-based flatbread) or boiled maize. 
• Vegetables: Steamed cabbage and carrots. 
• Proteins: Scrambled eggs or grilled chicken. 
• Fruits: Sliced avocado or tomato salad. 
• Dairy: A glass of milk or a serving of yogurt. 
4.7. Food Preparation Techniques to Maximize Nutrient Retention 
Proper food preparation techniques are crucial to preserving the nutritional value of 
foods. Overcooking or improper storage can lead to nutrient loss. Key practices 
include: 
• Cooking Methods: Use steaming, boiling, and grilling over frying or deep-frying 
to preserve vitamins and minerals. 
• Minimal Water Usage : When boiling vegetables like carrots or beets, use 
minimal water and reuse the water in soups to retain nutrients. 
• Gentle Handling: Avoid peeling vegetables excessively (e.g., potatoes), as much 
of the nutrient content is close to the skin. 
                                                                                               
 31 
• Balanced Cooking Time: Overcooking leafy greens like cabbage reduces their 
vitamin content. Cook lightly to preserve nutrients.
